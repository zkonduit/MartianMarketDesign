# Martian Market: A Living Design Fiction

## A world people can enter

Martian Market is an exercise in design fiction: we investigate a possible
future by building an economy and inhabiting the circumstances that give it
meaning. Participants join a growing Martian settlement, develop relationships,
negotiate exchanges, and influence its institutions. The experience combines
collaborative roleplaying, facilitated events, and an operational market.

The resources begin with things people actually offer: vegetables, repairs,
teaching, art, software, equipment, and time. A fictional role gives an exchange
another meaning. Repairing a bicycle might become maintaining a colony rover;
a gardening workshop might contribute to the settlement's agricultural history.
The real delivery terms remain explicit alongside that interpretation.

[martian.market](https://martian.market) is the project's existing prototype,
as reported by its creator. Its current interface and capabilities have not
been independently inspected for this draft. The market behavior described
below is a proposed direction for development and play.

The following setting supplies provisional shared history. Participants can
change its future through their actions. Scientific and historical references
provide constraints and analogies; they do not predict these events. The vector
economy remains an experimental hypothesis whose difficulties belong inside
the fiction.

## 2048: a settlement with several beginnings

By 2048, several dozen people, approaching a hundred as arrivals accumulate,
occupy connected habitats on Mars. Permanence is an intention that each failed
component puts to the test.

Earlier space-agency missions surveyed sites, installed instruments, and
established temporary outposts. Private expeditions subsequently brought cargo,
power systems, robots, and people prepared to stay longer. Some expeditions
failed; some passengers died without reaching the settlement. Survivors dispute
whether particular losses resulted from unavoidable uncertainty, bad equipment,
or promises made to investors.

An Earth-based Charter Association recruited a community before most members
could travel. Its financiers remain largely on Earth. Residents include
scientists and sponsored technicians, mechanics, medical workers, growers, and
adventurers. They are generally poorer than their backers, while possessing
skills that make passage possible. Contracts offer wages, future ownership,
or service commitments; a return journey remains uncertain.

Efficient Earth-to-Mars launch opportunities occur approximately every 26
months. Return opportunities depend on the mission, and a suitable trajectory
still requires a functioning vehicle, propellant, and a seat. These constraints
make departure a serious political question. [NASA mission logistics](https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/small-steps-giant-leaps/small-steps-giant-leaps-episode-71-mars-oxygen-in-situ-resource-utilization-experiment/).

The agencies remember public investment. Financiers remember taking financial
risks. Residents remember keeping everyone alive. Each account becomes a claim
to founding authority.

Robots perform much outdoor work, but their maintenance creates dependencies
on people, energy, and replacement parts. Minerals in the ground offer little
immediate help without extraction and processing capacity. Medicines,
electronics, agricultural inputs, and reliable machinery remain bottlenecks.
Dust threatens machinery and sunlight-dependent power; Mars's thin atmosphere
makes its storms different from terrestrial hurricanes. [NASA on Martian dust](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/the-fact-and-fiction-of-martian-dust-storms/).

## The Earth people leave behind

Earth remains habitable, beautiful, and unequal. In this setting, climate
damage, deforestation, displacement, financial pressures, and regional conflict
coexist with restoration projects and prosperous cities. Some people can buy
protection from disruption; others repeatedly rebuild their lives. The IPCC's
assessment of interacting risks and unequal vulnerability informs this
background. [IPCC synthesis](https://report.ipcc.ch/ar6syr/headline.html).

Residents leave for opportunity, research, adventure, belonging, or frustration
with institutions. Many love Earth and send money home. Their families may
regard the settlement as courageous, wasteful, or an escape available to a
fortunate minority. Earth remains physically easier to inhabit than Mars.

Monetary reformers argue that dependence on Earth currencies imports decisions
in which Martians have little voice. They particularly criticize US fiscal
policy and dollar dominance. Their opponents point to the financing, liquidity,
and public institutions that made settlement possible.

The dollar itself emerged through institutional changes: national banknotes,
the Federal Reserve, Bretton Woods, and the ending of official dollar-gold
convertibility. Financial instability and political conflict shaped those
changes. [Federal Reserve history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/federal-reserve-history),
[Bretton Woods history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/bretton-woods-launched).

Foreign societies are not automatically liable for US public debt. Dollar
borrowing, import invoicing, interest rates, and exchange-rate movements can
nevertheless transmit substantial costs. Those mechanisms give the fictional
grievance substance without settling its political interpretation.
[IMF on dollar spillovers](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2022/10/14/how-countries-should-respond-to-the-strong-dollar).

Mars uses local vector money while still needing Earth currencies for imports.
Research contracts, software, sponsorship, and intellectual property provide
possible external income. Profitable mineral exports remain a disputed
investment thesis. Local monetary invention cannot supply an absent component.

## A village that keeps growing

Initially, most residents know one another. They remember favors, competence,
carelessness, and sacrifice. The village can coordinate through conversation,
but personal knowledge also sustains grudges and favoritism. Dunbar's number is
a loose reference for this social scale; research disputes a precise universal
threshold. [Lindenfors, Wartel, and Lind](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8103230/).

Recruitment changes the world. New participants normally introduce resident
characters, real offers, and needs. Facilitators record their fictional
arrivals, relationships, and circumstances. Membership alone does not create
habitats or supplies: expansion requires agreements about capacity, training,
and provisioning within the shared story.

Growth brings benefits and tensions. A newcomer may provide a scarce skill
while knowing nobody who can vouch for them. Founders possess long transaction
histories; later arrivals challenge rules written before they had a voice.
Work becomes specialized, neighborhoods form, and people exchange with
counterparties they have never met.

Reputation then requires procedures for evidence and correction. Assemblies
need representation. Shared infrastructure requires budgets, maintenance
obligations, and dispute resolution. Further settlements may develop different
monetary practices and negotiate between them. These developments follow
participation and events rather than a fixed population ceiling.

Arrivals can be grouped into facilitated launch windows, allowing the community
to welcome recruits and decide what their arrival changes. Between events,
participants continue ordinary exchanges. A shared chronicle records decisions,
disagreements, and consequences; revised rules take effect prospectively on
announced terms. Growth thus creates an ongoing campaign whose history belongs
to the people participating in it.

## Property, authority, and independence

The settlement resembles an extreme network-state project: a community forms
online, gathers resources, occupies physical locations, and seeks recognition.
Diplomatic recognition is itself part of the network-state proposal. Próspera's
own description, by comparison, places it within Honduras's political
structure. [The Network State](https://thenetworkstate.com/the-network-state-in-one-essay),
[Próspera's account](https://help.prospera.co/en/articles/myth-busting-faq).

Ownership and government overlap awkwardly. A company owns a habitat, an agency
owns its instruments, residents operate its life-support equipment, and the
Association claims authority through its charter. Earth-based members argue
that distance should not erase their founding rights. Residents argue that
sharing the danger gives them a stronger claim to govern everyday life.

The present Outer Space Treaty prohibits national appropriation of celestial
bodies, requires state supervision of private activity, and preserves
jurisdiction over registered space objects and their personnel. Distance does
not extinguish those rules. Martian independence would require a contested
legal and diplomatic development. [Outer Space Treaty, Articles II, VI and VIII](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4225/documentation/cooperation/treaty.htm).

Earth retains influence through launch services, finance, suppliers, and assets
held there. Residents possess operational control and knowledge that distant
managers need. Neither side can simply dispense with the other.

The first institutions are small and overlap in personnel:

- A resident assembly negotiates local rules, membership, and provisioning.
- A mission commander exercises emergency powers with disputed limits.
- A provisioning committee maintains inventories and negotiates resupply.
- A ledger cooperative administers the monetary experiment and hears disputes.
- An Earth-based charter board controls funding and retains founding privileges.

Possible Earth interests include US scientific and financial influence; Chinese
industrial partnerships and technical standards; Indian engineering, services,
and diaspora networks; Russian specialist infrastructure; and European,
Japanese, Gulf, and other research or investment partnerships. These are
fictional possibilities. Governments, companies, scientists, and residents from
the same country frequently disagree.

Early American settlements offer analogies of company finance, dangerous
passage, and resupply dependence. Their histories also involved Indigenous
societies, dispossession, and coercion, which complicate any frontier mythology.
[Jamestown history](https://www.nps.gov/jame/learn/historyculture/a-short-history-of-jamestown.htm).
The Tea Party analogy highlights commercial privilege as well as taxation: the
Tea Act made company tea cheaper while strengthening the company's position.
A Martian sponsor might similarly offer affordable supplies in exchange for
exclusive contracts and political concessions. [Tea Party history](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/boston-tea-party-in-real-time.htm).

## Culture, contribution, and dependence

Communal meals adapt Earth traditions to limited ingredients. Repair work earns
prestige. Waste attracts criticism. Safety rituals preserve the memory of
accidents, while memorials retain names that investors would prefer associated
with progress. Communication delays encourage recorded letters and relationships
conducted across different moments of experience.

Privacy becomes scarce. Neighbors notice consumption, illness, and disagreements.
The person who keeps good records can expose misconduct or become an unofficial
judge. Residents disagree about which failures deserve forgiveness and which
records should follow someone into another settlement.

The founding charter ties resource entitlement to contribution. Its advocates
believe mutual dependence makes responsibility unavoidable. Yet the meaning of
contribution remains contested. Preventing an accident leaves less visible
evidence than repairing its damage. Caregiving takes time from recorded
production. A robot's owner receives returns while its maintainer bears risk.

An injured worker presents the hardest case. Past service, present need, future
capacity, and accumulated money suggest different entitlements. Residents may
organize gifts, insurance, exemptions, or rule changes; none is guaranteed by
the founding arrangement. These are conflicts within the fictional society.
Participants decide what real resources they offer and accept.

## Money whose meaning is learned

The central hypothesis is that transferable learned vectors can serve as money.
This extends beyond the archived model's use of embeddings to find compatible
trades. Monetary balances, reputation, physical inventories, and entitlement
rules remain distinct even when institutions bring them into the same decision.

For participant $p$, represent a balance as

$$
b_p \in \mathbb{R}^{d}.
$$

The coordinates have no prescribed everyday names. Interpretive projections
make aspects of the balance legible. A simple food projection is

$$
s_{\mathrm{food}}(b_p;c,t)=w_{\mathrm{food}}(c,t)^\top b_p,
$$

where $c$ records relevant context and $t$ time. Other projections describe
energy, repair, or estimated USD value. Scores require calibration; a food
score does not guarantee calories, and a USD estimate becomes a usable quote
only when someone offers to exchange on stated terms.

Within a shared model version, a payment vector $v$ could update balances:

$$
b_A'=b_A-v,\qquad b_B'=b_B+v.
$$

This preserves the aggregate vector for that payment. Issuance, credit, and
default require additional rules. Negative coordinates do not inherently mean
debt, and vector length supplies no universal ranking of wealth. Additive
accounting also assumes that the learned representation supports coherent
composition, something the experiment must establish.

The learning process could use offers, accepted exchanges, fulfillment, and
expressed preferences. Early data will be sparse and shaped by who joins first.
Research on unsupervised disentanglement shows that identifying underlying
factors generally requires assumptions about the models and data. Our inference
for the monetary experiment is that training data, calibration, and
interpretation become matters of governance. [Locatello and colleagues](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/locatello19a.html).

Model updates can change purchasing prospects. Pure coordinate changes require
consistent transformations of balances and projections; substantive changes in
valuation have distributional consequences. Participants need to recognize the
difference and contest the rules. Manipulated exchanges, unpopular newcomers,
and cheap labor accepted under pressure may all distort what the system learns.

Kocherlakota's account of money as memory motivates recording economic history
without proving that a learned vector currency will work. Remembering an
exchange also leaves questions about relevance, interpretation, and forgiveness.
[Money as memory](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/quarterly-review/the-technological-role-of-fiat-money).

An optimizer can search over delivery bundles and payment vectors satisfying
inventories, availability, deadlines, acceptance conditions, and agreed limits
on resulting wallet positions. It can expose competing objectives and
Pareto-efficient alternatives. Choosing among those alternatives
still requires preferences or collective rules. Physical deliveries, disputed
quality, and consent remain part of settlement. [Boyd and Vandenberghe](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/).

## Playing through a real exchange

The intended loop is to publish offers and needs, negotiate terms, inspect a
proposed match, authorize it, fulfill the commitments, and record outcomes.
Facilitators incorporate agreed consequences into shared history. Narrative
events change fictional circumstances; changes to real commitments require the
affected participants' agreement.

Before an experimental session uses vector payments, participants establish its
issuing authority, initial allocations, acceptance rules, and model version.
These choices are visible parts of the experiment. Recording delivery and
revising the fictional chronicle do not automatically mint money; the agreed
issuance policy determines when new balances enter circulation.

Consider three participants in the same Earth city. Mara offers a bicycle
repair, Idris offers a one-hour language lesson, and Lin offers a vegetable box.
Idris needs the repair, Lin wants the lesson, and Mara wants vegetables. They
agree on the repair scope, pickup location, lesson time, produce contents, and
delivery dates. The fictional counterparts are rover maintenance, communications
training, and greenhouse output.

Suppose each already holds $(5,5)$ in a toy two-coordinate currency. They
accept these payment vectors:

| Actual delivery | Payment | Balances after all three payments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mara repairs Idris's bicycle | Idris pays Mara $(2,1)$ | Mara: $(6,4)$ |
| Idris teaches Lin | Lin pays Idris $(1,1)$ | Idris: $(4,5)$ |
| Lin supplies Mara's vegetables | Mara pays Lin $(1,2)$ | Lin: $(5,6)$ |

The total remains $(15,15)$. The figures illustrate accounting after agreed
quotes; they do not derive a fair price from the coordinates. Each participant
considers both the goods received and the change in their wallet.

For illustration, choose food weights $(2,1)$, repair weights $(1,2)$, and USD
weights $(1,1)$. Mara's food score rises from 15 to 16, her repair score falls
from 15 to 14, and her USD score stays at 10. One scalar summary would miss
that change. These illustrative lenses provide scores; actual purchasing
opportunities still depend on available offers and accepted terms.

They agree when each payment becomes due and how delays or incomplete work
will be handled. Delivery confirmations record what actually happened; the
fiction then records repaired transport, completed training, and food supplied.
A fictional storm cannot undo a completed Earth repair. If someone leaves the
game, their real outstanding commitments still require an agreed resolution.

## An opening event: the missing shipment

A fictional resupply vehicle fails to arrive. A mechanic injured during earlier
repairs has declining recognized contributions, while neighbors remember that
their work saved the water system. An Earth investor offers replacement
supplies conditional on exclusive procurement and tighter oversight.

The assembly must allocate reserves, interpret the mechanic's entitlement, and
negotiate assistance. The ledger cooperative is also proposing an update that
changes the food projection of existing balances. Residents disagree over
whether it reveals scarcity or redistributes purchasing power.

Facilitators provide the circumstances and record decisions. Participants may
organize real exchanges, voluntary support, technical work, or a charter
proposal. Consequences become shared history and affect subsequent arrivals.
The outcome is left to play.

## Questions the experiment keeps open

- Can learned vector balances remain understandable and acceptable as money?
- How does the same balance or offer change value across circumstances?
- Who may issue money, authorize credit, or change monetary interpretations as
  the community grows?
- Which contributions become visible, and whose work disappears from the data?
- How can newcomers trade before they accumulate a history?
- When does coordination through familiarity require representative institutions?
- What happens when settlements learn incompatible monetary conventions?
- How do local monetary autonomy and dependence on Earth coexist?
